A visitor types a life question in English, Tamil, or Thanglish. The system finds the genuinely relevant verses among all 1,330 of the Thirukkural — even when the question shares no words with the text, and even across languages — and writes a short answer using only those verses, where every claim carries the number of the verse it rests on.

1,330

Two-line couplets on ethics, governance and love, written around two thousand years ago. Rigidly structured: 133 chapters of exactly ten verses each.

Each retrieved verse is shown with its Tamil original, transliteration, English translations, three modern Tamil prose readings and two classical commentaries. A citation the model invents — a verse number it was never given — is dropped before display and written to the log, because a citation that leads nowhere is a lie the reader can click.

The repository's own opening line is that it is a classroom, not a delivery project: the working product is a side effect, and the actual deliverable is understanding every line in it. That framing is why this page can be this specific.

The harness came first

Before any retrieval code existed there were 233 questions with hand-checked answer keys, in two sets that disagree with each other on purpose.

The honest set is 100 questions, and its answer key is not just "the chapter this question belongs to". It also carries 200 hand-checked verses from other chapters that genuinely answer the question — each one proposed by a keyword sweep, then read and judged.

The worked example. Verse 35 — "Four ills eschew: lust, anger, envy, evil-speech" — genuinely answers a question about anger, but lives in chapter 4, not the anger chapter 31. Without the hand-checked extras the scorecard would have punished a retriever for being right.

The second set is 133 questions, one per chapter, in deliberately awkward real-world phrasings — "my classmate got the promotion and I feel bitter". It is stricter in absolute terms and biased toward chapter-matching methods by construction, which is exactly why results get split by set before they get believed.

How a question becomes an answer

Four stages. Every constant in them was set by a measurement, not a default.

Every gain measured on its own

One change at a time, each verified with McNemar's exact test — the paired test that looks only at the questions two methods disagree on, because if the methods were equally good each disagreement is a fair coin flip. Below 0.05 the project says the effect is real; otherwise it is not established and the change does not ship.

Computed by hand once, to check the harness: a 9-vs-1 split in 10 disagreements gives 22 lopsided outcomes of 1,024, so p = 0.0215 — reproducing the number the harness had printed that morning.

The 85→90 step could not be established at 100 questions (p = 0.125) and needed the set grown to 233 to settle at p = 0.0129 — the first properly powered experiment here, run after a power calculation said about 200 questions would decide it.

The largest single gain in the whole project was free: deleting about 111 question and function words from the query took the golden set from 44 to 69. No information was added. Noise was removed.

26 / 100

Questions answered by one verse about eating meat — because its translation opens with a rhetorical question, and so did the queries. The model was matching the shape of the text, not its subject.

That failure is worth stating plainly, because it is the reason the rewriting stage exists at all. Keyword search had the same problem from the other side: BM25 weighted the word "how" at 3.78, nearly level with "anger" at 4.13, because old verse rarely says "how do I".

What I don't claim

Replacing the word list with a hosted rewriter beat it decisively. Replacing it with a local model beat it too. Between those two rewriters, the hosted one scored fifteen questions higher — and landed at p = 0.0534.

Just outside. Reporting it as a win would have been easy, and nobody would have checked. The hosted rewriter ships anyway, but on latency grounds — 0.5 s against roughly 100 s per question for the local model on a real host — which is a different argument, made openly rather than smuggled in behind a number.

One earlier result is void rather than merely unproven. A frontier model's query rewrites once scored 97 out of 100 — because it had the chapter list in its context and was echoing correct chapter titles back. It is kept in the log marked VOID, as an example of catching leakage, and never as a result. It is a different number from the 97 at the top of this page, which comes from the corpus modernisation and was verified at p = 0.0391.

Two things that were wrong before anything worked

The join

The corpus is built from two public files. One has all 1,330 verses but no Parimelazhagar commentary; the other has the commentary but is missing verses 395 and 648. Joining them on position rather than on the verse number would have silently mis-paired 934 of 1,330 records, because everything after a hole slides up by one.

934 / 1330

Nothing crashes, the output looks fine, and 70% of it is wrong. The archetypal bug shape in this project — every bug here has been silent.

The audit that found the rest — a truncated line in verse 1000, two verses carrying another verse's meaning, 664 records with an English translation glued onto the end of a Tamil commentary — was itself tested before it was trusted. Twenty-two errors were planted deliberately. The small multilingual embedding model caught none of them; LaBSE caught fifteen. That measurement is why the project uses LaBSE. A detector nobody has tested is worth nothing.

One defect is worth naming on its own: verse 524 carried verse 468's meaning in both raw sources and in two of three reference websites, because they all copy the same upstream dataset. Agreement between sources proves nothing when they copy one another.

The metric that graded its own homework

Defining "correct" as "in the right chapter" and then tuning the chapter weight is a metric rewarding the thing being tested. It said a chapter weight of 0.8 was best; the honest set said 0.8 scored 42 — below the 44 baseline. Five weeks later the same trap fired again, with the written warning sitting in the code and read that same morning. Reading a lesson is not owning it.

Correlation, then intervention

Counting how many correct verses reached the candidate pile produced a beautiful curve: success climbed with the count and never flattened. The causal story felt obvious — give the reranker more to work with and it does better.

So the pile was widened from 50 to 75. The count went up, from 7.2 correct verses per question to 8.2, and the score went down, 174 to 165, at p = 0.0352. The extra candidates were distractors this reranker fell for. The correlation reading was dead and the reranker was the wall; replacing it moved rank-1 from 105 to 131 out of 233 at p = 0.0001.

p = 0.0001

The strongest result in the project, and it only exists because the intervention contradicted the correlation. A plausible mechanism is not a cause.

The replacement was run on a rented T4 GPU through a portable package whose 50-candidate piles were frozen at home and shipped as data — different machines' embeddings differ in the last decimals, enough to swap candidate 50 for 51 and void the comparison. The package was validated first by running the shipping model through it and reproducing the saved baseline with zero disagreements.

Where it stands

Retrieval is done and scored. Generation is wired end to end with citations checked against the verses the model was actually given, but the breaking tests — deliberately feeding it wrong verses, off-topic questions, and hunting invented citations at scale — have not been run, so the honest description is that it is citation-checked but not yet adversarially tested.

Citation validity is unmeasured, and the app renders it as a blank rather than an estimate. Measuring it needs a judge, and three judges compared on 113 claims disagreed too much to trust: the model judging its own answers called 92% of them clean, a different vendor's model called 73.5% clean, and the two disagreed on 30 of the 113 — one of them calling a word-for-word quote unsupported. A model cannot judge its own answers, and an unchecked instrument is not a measurement.

The refusal thresholds read "planned" for the same reason. The measurement said the score cannot yet separate a question the book answers from one it doesn't — a floor set anywhere useful would refuse four genuine questions to catch one fake. So the interface ships with confidence uncalibrated and says so, rather than claiming certainty on the engine's behalf.

The most honest thing about the current state is the ceiling: the golden set's best score is 97 of 100, which leaves three questions of room, so a real improvement and no improvement now produce the same verdict. The limit is the test set, not the pipeline. The ruler, not the model, is what needs to grow.
