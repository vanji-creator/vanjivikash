About

I came to machine learning through shipping, not through a course.

Which is why the first thing I build is always the thing that tells me whether the rest of it works.

For two years I have been the person who owns a national platform's backend, its security posture and its releases. That job teaches you a specific kind of caution: you cannot tell whether something is working by looking at it, and the failures that hurt are the ones that do not announce themselves.

How I got here

I finished a B.Tech in Computer Science at Amrita Vishwa Vidyapeetham in 2024 and joined C-DAC Chennai as a project associate. The work was full-stack and then quickly more than that: React and Next.js front-ends, a Python and FastAPI backend, an analytics layer, an integration with an external logistics system, and eventually the security hardening and release engineering that decide whether any of it is allowed to run.

C-DAC is a government R&D organisation. The platform I work on serves about 2,500 people and had to earn CERT-In "Safe to Host" certification before it could go live.

The move toward machine learning started with a Chrome extension. I wanted to know whether a link was dangerous before clicking it, which sounds like a classification problem and mostly is. My first attempt was a fine-tuned transformer, and it was good enough to fool me for a while — until a domain-grouped evaluation showed it had learned which hosts appeared in which class rather than anything about URLs.

Throwing that away and shipping a logistic regression instead is the moment the rest of this makes sense from. The lesson was not "small models are better." It was that I had had no way of knowing which model was better until I built one, and that building it should have come first.

How I work

Two habits, both borrowed and both kept because they have caught me being wrong.

Predict before you run

Before an experiment, I write down what I expect to happen. The gap between the prediction and the result is where the learning is, and several of the predictions on this site were wrong on purpose — the widened candidate pile that made retrieval worse is the clearest one.

Explain it back

A concept does not count as learned until I can rebuild it from scratch. Cosine similarity got computed on paper with toy vectors before NumPy was allowed near it; BM25 was derived from three plain-language ideas before its formula was named; retrieval was a loop over all 1,330 verses before it was anything faster.

The corollary is deleting things. An approximate-nearest-neighbour index was removed from Kural RAG entirely: at 1,330 verses an exact scan takes 4 ms, so the index would have traded real accuracy for speed nobody needed.

The thread through both is the same as the thread through the security work: a claim nothing checks is a wish. If a rule matters, something automated has to enforce it, because a plan to be careful is not a control.
